When I had my third baby, I remember sitting in the dim light of my hospital room, completely exhausted, trying to figure out the whole breastfeeding thing. My nipples were sore, my baby was fussy, and I felt like I was fumbling in the dark. Have you ever felt that overwhelming pressure to just "get it right" immediately after birth? I know I have.

As a certified birth doula and mother of seven, I've seen this at births time and time again. Breastfeeding is natural, but it doesn't always come naturally. There are so many factors that play into how those first few feeds go. But recently, I came across some fascinating research that points to something you might not expect: the type of maternity care provider you choose during your pregnancy might actually influence your early breastfeeding rates.

Let's sit down and talk about what this means for you, your baby, and how you put together your care team.

## The Big Picture: Who Cares for You Matters

A massive retrospective cohort study looked at all the women who gave birth in Victoria, Australia, over a ten-year period from 2010 to 2020. That is a huge amount of data! The researchers wanted to know if the type of maternity care provider a woman had made a difference in whether she successfully breastfed in those early days. They tracked whether women were exclusively breastfeeding, partially breastfeeding, or not breastfeeding at all when they left the hospital.

The evidence indicates that it absolutely does make a difference. The study found that models emphasizing continuity of midwifery care are generally associated with improved early breastfeeding outcomes compared to fragmented or purely obstetric-led models. In plain English? Moms who had continuous midwifery care had better early breastfeeding rates than those who saw different doctors or rotated through a clinic where they never knew who would be at their birth.

I had a client a few years ago—we'll call her Maya. With her first baby, she went to a large obstetric practice. She saw a different doctor at every visit. By the time she went into labor, she felt like just another number. After her baby was born, she struggled with breastfeeding and felt like the rotating staff didn't really know her or her goals. With her second baby, Maya switched to a midwifery practice where she saw the same midwife the whole time. She told me that because her midwife already knew her feeding goals before she even arrived at the hospital, the early support she received was completely different. Her breastfeeding journey started off so much smoother.

**What this means for you:**
When you are deciding where to get your prenatal care, remember that this choice extends beyond just the birth. It can impact your postpartum experience, too. You might want to look into care models that offer continuity, where you see the same provider or a small team throughout your pregnancy.

## What is Continuity of Midwifery Care?

You might be wondering what "continuity of midwifery care" actually looks like. It simply means that you are cared for by a known midwife (or a small group of midwives) throughout your pregnancy, during your labor and birth, and into the early postpartum period. Instead of seeing a different face at every appointment, you build a relationship with one primary care provider.

Fragmented care, on the other hand, is what happens when you see whichever doctor is on duty at your clinic. You might see Dr. Smith one month, Dr. Jones the next, and then whoever happens to be on call when you actually go into labor. While this model works for managing medical complications, the research suggests it falls short when it comes to things like early breastfeeding support. When you don't know who will catch your baby, it's hard to build the deep trust that helps you feel relaxed and supported after birth.

If you're trying to understand the difference between a midwife and a doctor, or even how a doula fits into the picture, you can check out our guide on [Doula vs Midwife](/doula-vs-midwife/). Knowing the roles of each team member helps you build the best possible support system.

**What this means for you:**
As you interview clinics or practices, ask them directly about their care model. Do they offer midwifery-led care where you see the same person? If they only offer a rotating physician model, you can decide if that aligns with your goals or if you want to look elsewhere.

## Why Does Seeing the Same Provider Help Breastfeeding?

Why would seeing the same midwife make your baby latch better or your milk come in easier? It all comes down to trust, stress, and personalized education.

When you see the same provider for nine months, they get to know you. They know your fears, your goals, and your history. The study from Victoria found that this relationship pays off in the postpartum period. Evidence indicates that when a mother feels safe and supported, her stress hormones stay lower. High levels of stress can interfere with the release of oxytocin, the hormone that helps your milk let down and helps you bond with your baby. When you are laboring and recovering with a provider you know and trust, your body feels safer, which can make breastfeeding physically easier.

Plus, a midwife who has been talking with you about breastfeeding since your first trimester can tailor their education to exactly what you need. They know if you have inverted nipples, if you had a difficult time with a previous baby, or if you are planning to go back to work early. They can prepare you specifically, rather than giving you a generic handout on your way out the door.

**What this means for you:**
Building a relationship with your care provider isn't just about having a friendly chat. It is a physical, physiological advantage for your body. Take the time to discuss your breastfeeding goals early and often with a provider who will actually be there to help you achieve them.

## Questions to bring to your care team

- Do you offer a model of care where I will see the same provider (or small team) throughout my pregnancy and birth?
- How do you support early breastfeeding in the first few hours after my baby is born?
- If I have a cesarean or complications, how does my feeding plan change, and who will help me?
- Can I meet with a lactation consultant before I give birth to prepare?
