I remember with my third baby, I was so exhausted that just taking a shower felt like a massive chore. When my husband hinted at being intimate a few weeks after my six-week checkup, I just looked at him. I love my husband, but my body felt like it belonged to the baby, and my brain was running on zero sleep.

If you're expecting a baby right now, you might not be thinking about postpartum intimacy yet. But if you've recently had a baby and you're just not feeling it, I want you to know you are completely normal. Mama, if you’re just not feeling in the mood for intimacy after having your baby, research suggests you are completely normal.

A recent study published in PubMed looked at low sexual desire in postpartum women. The research found that a low sex drive postpartum is often tied to how you're feeling emotionally and how your body is physically healing. It isn't just all in your head. Your body just did an incredible amount of work, and it takes time to adjust.

I had a client a few years ago, let's call her Sarah. After her first baby, she felt so much pressure to get back to normal intimacy. She told me she felt broken because she had zero desire, even months after birth. We talked about how much her body had just done, and I reminded her that healing isn't just about making it to the six-week mark. Once she gave herself permission to wait until she actually felt ready, the pressure lifted.

## Your Body Needs Time to Physically Heal
When we talk about sex after a baby, we have to talk about physical recovery first. The research I mentioned found that physical sexual function plays a huge role in your desire. If something hurts, you're not going to want to do it. It's that simple.

Think about what your body just went through. Whether you pushed a baby out or had a cesarean, you have a wound that needs to heal. If you had any tearing, scar tissue needs time to soften and remodel. If you're breastfeeding, your estrogen levels are often very low, which can lead to vaginal dryness and tissue thinning. All of these physical changes can make intimacy uncomfortable or even painful.

Evidence indicates that women who experience pain during sex postpartum are much more likely to report low desire. Your body is trying to protect you from pain, so it naturally lowers your drive. It's a protective mechanism, not a defect. Your pelvic floor muscles stretched immensely to make room for your baby, and they need gentle, gradual recovery. Doing too much too soon can set you back.

**What this means for you:** Give yourself plenty of grace as you adjust to life with a newborn. If you try to have sex and it hurts, stop. Do not push through pain. Consider chatting with your care provider about any physical discomfort you might be feeling. Pelvic floor physical therapy is a wonderful tool if you're dealing with persistent pain or tension.

## The Emotional Toll on Your Drive
The study also highlighted psychosocial factors. This means your mental and emotional state deeply affects your sex drive. How are you sleeping? Are you feeling anxious? Are you struggling with your new identity as a mom?

When you are in survival mode, just trying to keep a tiny human fed and alive, your brain does not prioritize intimacy. Stress and fatigue are the biggest mood killers out there. The research suggests that emotional overwhelm, including postpartum depression and anxiety, is heavily linked to low desire. When your brain is flooded with stress hormones, it suppresses the hormones that control your sex drive.

It makes sense, right? If you don't feel safe and relaxed, your body isn't going to be ready for intimacy. You need to feel emotionally grounded before you can connect with a partner in that way. On top of that, you are navigating a massive identity shift. You are still you, but you are also a mother now. That changes how you see yourself and how you relate to your partner.

**What this means for you:** Check in with your emotions. If you're feeling constantly overwhelmed, weepy, or disconnected, tell someone. Consider chatting with your care provider about any emotional overwhelm you might be feeling. There is no shame in needing extra support, whether that's therapy, medication, or just a few hours of uninterrupted sleep.

## The Myth of the Six-Week Green Light
We put so much pressure on that six-week postpartum checkup. It feels like a finish line. But the truth is, six weeks is just a medical clearance to say you probably won't get an infection from intercourse. It is not a magic switch that flips your sex drive back on.

Some women feel ready at four weeks. Some women don't feel ready for a year. Both are normal. The study shows that low desire is common well past the initial postpartum period. Your hormones are shifting, your routine is completely different, and your relationship with your partner is adjusting to a new dynamic.

Whether you are seeing an OB or exploring the differences between a [Doula vs Midwife](/doula-vs-midwife/), remember that your care team is there to support your whole health. Don't let a calendar dictate your readiness. Your body's timeline is unique to you.

**What this means for you:** Throw out the timeline. Talk to your partner about the fact that the six-week mark is just a check-in, not a deadline. You get to decide when you feel physically and emotionally ready to try intimacy again.

## Communicating With Your Partner
One of the hardest parts of low desire is figuring out how to talk about it with your partner. They might feel rejected, and you might feel guilty. But keeping quiet only makes it worse.

The research points out that relationship dynamics are part of those psychosocial factors. If you and your partner are disconnected or arguing, your drive will drop even more. Intimacy isn't just about sex. It's about feeling close and understood.

Sit down with your partner when you're both calm. Explain what the research says. Tell them, 'My body is healing, my hormones are shifting, and I am exhausted. It's not that I don't love you. I just need time.' Find other ways to connect. Hold hands, take a walk, or just sit together on the couch without any pressure for things to lead to sex. A back rub or a long hug can keep you connected while your body heals.

**What this means for you:** Talk openly with your partner. Share this article with them if it helps. Make an agreement that intimacy will happen when you both feel ready, without pressure or guilt.

## When to Ask for Extra Help
While low desire is normal, sometimes it points to something we need to address. If you're dealing with severe pain, pelvic floor dysfunction, or signs of postpartum depression or anxiety, it's time to bring in the professionals.

Don't wait for your six-week checkup if you're struggling emotionally. You can call your doctor or midwife anytime. If you need continuous emotional support, a postpartum doula is a great resource. They can help you process your birth, care for the baby so you can sleep, and point you toward local therapists if you need one.

**What this means for you:** Don't suffer in silence. If your lack of desire is causing you distress, or if you're dealing with pain or deep sadness, reach out. Your health matters just as much as your baby's health.

## Questions to Bring to Your Care Team
When you go to your postpartum appointments, bring a list of questions. It's easy to forget what you wanted to ask when you're sitting in the office. Here are a few to get you started:

* I am experiencing pain during sex. What are my options for treatment or pelvic floor therapy?
* I have very little to no sex drive. Is this normal for how far postpartum I am?
* I'm feeling overwhelmed and anxious. Can we talk about my mental health?
* Could my breastfeeding routine be affecting my vaginal tissue, and what can I do about it?
* When should I be concerned about my lack of desire, and what are the next steps?
